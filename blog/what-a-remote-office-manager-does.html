What a remote office manager actually does for a small business

A realistic job description for a full-time remote office manager at a U.S. small business, what to hand off first, and what to expect in the first 90 days.

"Office manager" means something different at every small business. At a pest control company it is scheduling and customer calls. At a law firm it is intake and billing. At a SaaS startup it is ops and the thing nobody else owns. Here is what the role looks like when it is remote, and how to make it work.

What a remote office manager can own

  • Scheduling and calendar. Appointments, jobs, meetings, confirmations, reschedules, and the follow-up when someone no-shows.
  • Phone and inbox. First response on calls and email, routing what needs a human on site, closing out what does not.
  • Vendors and orders. Supplies, software renewals, equipment, and keeping the list of who you owe what.
  • Billing and collections. Sending invoices, posting payments, chasing past-dues, keeping the books clean for your accountant.
  • People admin. Timesheets, PTO tracking, onboarding paperwork for new hires, keeping the handbook current.
  • Reporting. The weekly numbers you keep meaning to look at: jobs booked, revenue by service, outstanding AR, review count.
  • The hundred small things. Renewing the business license, updating the Google listing, ordering the holiday cards, finding out why the printer stopped. Not the printer, obviously. But everything else.

What it cannot own

Anything physical. Signing for deliveries, walking a customer to the back, restocking the truck. If a big part of your office manager's day is physical, you either need a local hire or you need to split the role, with a remote office manager running everything on a screen and a part-time local person handling the building.

What to hand off first

Start with the thing that costs you the most when it slips. For most owners that is billing. For a lot of service businesses it is the phone. Pick one, hand it over fully in the first two weeks, and add the next area once that one is stable. Do not hand over everything on day one; nobody can absorb a whole business in a week.

The first 90 days

Days 1 to 30: systems, people, and one or two owned areas. Daily ten-minute check-ins.

Days 31 to 60: most of the admin is theirs. They start spotting problems before you do. Check-ins drop to twice a week.

Days 61 to 90: they are running the office. You are getting a weekly summary and a short list of decisions that need you. This is when owners tell us they got their evenings back.

What to look for in the person

Organized, obviously. But the thing that separates great office managers from adequate ones is judgment: knowing which fire to put out first, which customer needs the owner, and which question can wait until tomorrow. Questions we get about this

How is a remote office manager different from a virtual assistant?

A VA is usually part-time, task-based, and shared across clients. A Hybryd office manager is a full-time member of your team who owns outcomes, not tasks, and is supported by a talent manager for the long term.

Can a remote office manager supervise other remote team members?

Yes, and that is a common path. Many of our partners' office managers coordinate the support rep and the AR clerk as the team grows.

What software should they know?

Whatever you use. Common ones: Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, QuickBooks, your industry platform, a VoIP phone system, and Slack or Teams. We screen for your stack specifically.
